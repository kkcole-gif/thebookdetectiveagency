THE STORY

A six year old who reads anything and writes one word.

The Agency started as a game for one child. It turned out the child was not unusual.

My son taught himself to read before kindergarten and has not stopped. Chapter books at five, series at six, the kind of child who reads the cereal box, the parking sign, and the fine print on the back of a receipt. Ask him to write about any of it and he produces one word, a scowl, and a negotiation.

This fall a school writing assessment made the gap official. His reading score was fine. His writing score was not, and the letter home took care to say he was a strong writer for his age. Both things were true at once. He reads years ahead and writes on the ordinary clock, and the ordinary clock is slower. I teach writing for a living, and I had spent a decade telling college students the same thing without quite seeing it at my own table.

So I made him a game. Every book is a case. A case gets a file: a drawing of the evidence, three to five lines of report, and a stamp. A sand timer he turns himself. A card he draws that tells him what kind of detective work the case needs. A binder nobody else writes in, no corrections, no gold stars. Rank by number of files, never by quality, never taken away. On a hard day the Fairness Clause applies, and he wrote that clause himself, in pencil, with one spelling I am not allowed to fix.

The children I had in mind while making the kit were not only mine. The child in a gifted program who reads under the desk. The homeschooled child whose parent has run out of worksheets. The child with ADHD who refuses anything that looks like being managed. The child whose hand tires before the idea is out. The child reading in a second language and writing in it with dread. The same gap, different reasons. The kit on this site is the version of the game I would hand any of them.

What the Agency believes

Reading and writing are reciprocal: two skills with one base, each strengthening the other, maturing on different clocks. Children who read far ahead of their writing are common, and they are not behind. Writing on demand develops later than reading, and it develops on volume: short, frequent, low-stakes, uncorrected, and about something the child has read, so that the writing feeds the reading back. Choice matters more than reward. A ten minute limit builds more stamina than an open-ended session, because a child who stops wanting more comes back. And the child who writes the rules follows them.

Every one of those claims has a study behind it. They are on the research page, linked by DOI.
