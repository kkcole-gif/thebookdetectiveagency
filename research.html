FOR TEACHERS, AND FOR PARENTS WHO READ FOOTNOTES

The research behind the Agency

Every rule in the game comes from a finding. This page lists the finding, links the study, and says what the Agency does with it. Twelve sources, all peer reviewed or federally reviewed, each linked by DOI or to its publisher. Nothing here is a claim the Agency has tested on its own; the pilot is where that begins.

THE PREMISE

Reading and writing are separate skills with a shared base.

A child who reads well has one skill. A child who writes well has another. They draw on the same knowledge of language and the world, and they develop on different schedules, which is why a strong reader can stall at the pencil. The Agency treats the gap as ordinary and builds the bridge from the reading side.

Fitzgerald, J., and Shanahan, T. (2000). Reading and writing relations and their development. Educational Psychologist, 35(1), 39–50. https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3501_5Reading and writing share knowledge at four levels, from print to procedures, while remaining distinct processes whose relationship changes as children develop.
THE ENGINE

Writing about a text improves understanding of it.

Each card sends the child back into the book to find something, then asks for it on paper. That is the move the evidence supports most strongly: extended writing about what one has read, more than answering questions or taking notes, raises comprehension, and the effect holds in the elementary grades.

Graham, S., and Hebert, M. (2011). Writing to read: A meta-analysis of the impact of writing and writing instruction on reading. Harvard Educational Review, 81(4), 710–744. https://doi.org/10.17763/haer.81.4.t2k0m13756113566Across 95 studies, having students write about texts they read, teaching writing, and increasing how much students write each improved reading comprehension and fluency.
Graham, S., and Hebert, M. (2010). Writing to read: Evidence for how writing can improve reading. A Carnegie Corporation report. Washington, DC: Alliance for Excellent Education. carnegie.orgThe practitioner version of the same analysis, with three instructional practices: have students write about what they read, teach the skills and processes of writing, and increase how much students write.
ONE CASE A DAY

Children need daily writing time, and the amount matters.

The federal practice guide for elementary writing puts daily writing time first among its four recommendations and sets a minimum of thirty minutes a day from first grade on. The Agency asks for ten minutes a day at home, on top of school, and holds the line at daily because the research holds it there.

Graham, S., Bollinger, A., Booth Olson, C., D'Aoust, C., MacArthur, C., McCutchen, D., and Olinghouse, N. (2012, revised 2018). Teaching elementary school students to be effective writers: A practice guide (NCEE 2012-4058). Washington, DC: Institute of Education Sciences, What Works Clearinghouse. ies.ed.govRecommendation 1: provide daily time for students to write. Recommendation 4: create an engaged community of writers, which is the rule that grown-ups file cases too.
THREE TO FIVE LINES, STOP AT THE SAND

Writing skill grows through deliberate, bounded practice.

Composing is one of the most demanding things a young mind does, because planning, generating, and transcribing compete for the same limited attention. Short daily sessions with a clear endpoint are how that load becomes manageable. The timer is the boundary. Stopping while the child still wants to continue protects tomorrow's session.

Kellogg, R. T. (2008). Training writing skills: A cognitive developmental perspective. Journal of Writing Research, 1(1), 1–26. https://doi.org/10.17239/jowr-2008.01.01.1Writing expertise develops over years through deliberate practice that reduces the working-memory cost of composing, in the same way expertise develops in music and chess.
DRAW THE EVIDENCE, DICTATE ON A HARD DAY

Handwriting and spelling constrain what young children can compose.

For a six year old, the hand is often the bottleneck. When the mechanics of forming letters consume attention, the ideas never reach the page, and the gap between what a child can say and what a child can write looks like a thinking problem when it is a transcription problem. The evidence box on the case file, and the Fairness Clause's permission to dictate, both exist because of this finding.

Graham, S., Berninger, V. W., Abbott, R. D., Abbott, S. P., and Whitaker, D. (1997). Role of mechanics in composing of elementary school students: A new methodological approach. Journal of Educational Psychology, 89(1), 170–182. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.89.1.170Handwriting fluency and spelling accounted for a large share of the variance in composition quality and length in the primary grades, with handwriting the stronger constraint early on.
THE CHILD PICKS THE BOOK AND DRAWS THE CARD

Choice raises intrinsic motivation, effort, and performance.

The child chooses the book, draws the card, turns the timer, and writes the Agency's rules. None of that is decoration. Meaningful choice is one of the best supported levers on a child's willingness to do hard work, and the effect is largest when the choices are few, real, and the child's own.

Patall, E. A., Cooper, H., and Robinson, J. C. (2008). The effects of choice on intrinsic motivation and related outcomes: A meta-analysis of research findings. Psychological Bulletin, 134(2), 270–300. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.270Across 41 studies, providing choice increased intrinsic motivation, effort, task performance, and perceived competence, with the strongest effects for children and for two to four options at a time.
Guthrie, J. T., Wigfield, A., Barbosa, P., Perencevich, K. C., Taboada, A., Davis, M. H., Scafiddi, N. T., and Tonks, S. (2004). Increasing reading comprehension and engagement through Concept-Oriented Reading Instruction. Journal of Educational Psychology, 96(3), 403–423. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.96.3.403Instruction that combined comprehension strategies with motivational supports, including student choice and hands-on engagement, raised comprehension, strategy use, and reading motivation in third grade over strategy instruction alone.
NO PRIZES, NO STREAKS, RANK BY COUNT ALONE

Tangible rewards for an activity reduce interest in the activity.

The Agency's ranks depend on the number of files and nothing else, and no rank is ever taken away. No stickers for good work, no prize at twenty, no streak to break. This is the most counterintuitive rule for adults and the one with the longest evidence trail: expected, tangible rewards for doing something undermine the wish to keep doing it once the reward stops.

Deci, E. L., Koestner, R., and Ryan, R. M. (1999). A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation. Psychological Bulletin, 125(6), 627–668. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627Across 128 experiments, expected tangible rewards reliably undermined intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks, with the strongest negative effects for children. Unexpected rewards and verbal feedback did not.
FOR THE CHILD WHO IS BORED IN THE READING BLOCK

Advanced readers lose nothing when they skip what they already know.

Parents of children who read far ahead often meet the same worry at school: if the child skips the grade-level work, the gaps will show. The compacting research tested that worry directly and found no cost. The Agency's classroom use is the independent work a compacted reading block needs, and the parent's guide asks for exactly that arrangement.

Reis, S. M., Westberg, K. L., Kulikowich, J. M., and Purcell, J. H. (1998). Curriculum compacting and achievement test scores: What does the research say? Gifted Child Quarterly, 42(2), 123–129. https://doi.org/10.1177/001698629804200206High-ability elementary students whose regular curriculum was reduced by 40 to 50 percent showed no decline in achievement test scores, including in reading, compared with peers who completed all of it.
Rogers, K. B. (2007). Lessons learned about educating the gifted and talented: A synthesis of the research on educational practice. Gifted Child Quarterly, 51(4), 382–396. https://doi.org/10.1177/0016986207306324Five lessons from the synthesis, among them daily challenge in the student's area of talent and opportunities for independent work in areas of interest.
EVERY PAGE IN LEXEND

Typeface shape and spacing change how well children with dyslexia read.

The kit is set entirely in Lexend, a sans-serif typeface designed around reading proficiency, with wide letters and generous spacing. The choice rests on the experimental finding that sans-serif, roman, and monospaced fonts improve reading performance for readers with dyslexia over italic and serif fonts, and that character spacing matters.

Rello, L., and Baeza-Yates, R. (2013). Good fonts for dyslexia. In Proceedings of the 15th International ACM SIGACCESS Conference on Computers and Accessibility (ASSETS '13), Article 14, 1–8. https://doi.org/10.1145/2513383.2513447In an eye-tracking study of 48 readers with dyslexia, sans-serif, monospaced, and roman fonts improved reading performance, while italic fonts hurt it. Lexend itself postdates the study; the kit applies its principles rather than its tested fonts.
WHAT THIS PAGE DOES NOT CLAIM

The Agency has not been studied. The pieces have.

Every rule above rests on published work. The combination is a design, built at a kitchen table by a writing professor for one six year old and now in pilot with a handful of families.
