TWO SKILLS, ONE GAME

Reading and writing are different skills. Every card uses both.

A child who reads well has one skill. A child who writes well has another. They share a knowledge base and develop on separate clocks, which is why a strong reader can stall at the pencil. Each card in the deck sends the child back into the book to find something, then asks for it on paper. Read to find. Write to tell.

THE READING HALF
Comprehension, evidence, inference
  • Finding the line that proves a point, and copying it out
  • Working out what a character wants from what they do
  • Spotting the clues an author planted before the turn
  • Predicting the next chapter, then checking
  • Holding two books side by side and comparing them
THE WRITING HALF
Opinion, narrative, structure, stamina
  • Stating a verdict and giving a reason for it
  • Writing the sequel's first sentence, or a scene from another character's eyes
  • Putting a story into four boxes, a map, a timeline
  • Three to five lines on demand, every day, until it is ordinary
  • Finishing when the sand runs out, so there is always a tomorrow

WHY IT WORKS

Reading and writing run on different clocks.

Many children read well ahead of what they can put on paper. Some are years ahead. Some have hands that tire before the idea is out. Some erase every line. Nothing is wrong with any of them. Composing on demand develops later than reading, and school tests tend to measure the slower one.

The Agency makes the writing theirs. A case they chose, a card they drew, a timer they turned, and a binder nobody corrects. Volume comes first. Accuracy follows volume.

Every page is set in Lexend, a typeface designed for readers with dyslexia. The Fairness Clause lets a child dictate, draw, or file a one-dot case on a hard day, so a detective who cannot hold a pencil for long is still a detective.
